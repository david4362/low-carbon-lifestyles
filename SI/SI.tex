\section*{S1. Data, recruitment, and ethical approvals}

Participants were recruited during Spring 2024 via Sweden's national digital mailbox service (\emph{Mina meddelanden}), to which around 85\% of the adult population is connected \citep{digg_mina_meddelanden_2025w48,scb_befolk_manad_ckm_2025}.
Invitations provided general information about a study on household consumption patterns and included a link to the baseline survey, followed by instructions for downloading the research app (\emph{Konsumtionskollen}). Participants were not informed in advance that individual carbon footprints would be analysed, reducing the risk of priming or selection based on environmental concern. The recruitment, consent, and data-handling procedures were covered by an approved ethics protocol; ethical approval was granted by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Ref.\ 2024-01766-02).

The sampling frame was drawn from Statistics Sweden population registers using two parallel invitation streams. (i)~A \emph{random-sample frame} of
approximately 80{,}000 individuals, drawn to be broadly representative of the Swedish single-adult household population. (ii)~A \emph{green oversample frame} of approximately 100{,}000 individuals, drawn from electoral districts (\emph{valkretsar}) in which the Green Party (Milj\"opartiet) received a substantially higher vote share than the national average. Together the two streams comprise an invitation frame of approximately 180{,}000 individuals. Eligibility required a smartphone with eID (BankID), account linkage via a secure banking interface, and consent for survey participation and registry linkage.

The green oversample was recruited using the same procedure and eligibility criteria as the random sample but is non-random by design. It is not part of the primary analytical sample; it serves only as an independent verification sample for the main results (Section~S13). All demographic benchmarks reported below therefore refer to the random-sample frame.

Of the approximately 80{,}000 individuals invited in the random-sample stream, 2{,}347 installed the app, linked at least one bank account, and consented to registry linkage. Table~\ref{tab:sample_flow} summarises the data-quality filters subsequently applied to this group, yielding a final analytical sample of 1{,}311 single-adult households---approximately 1.6\% of the random-sample invitations.

\section*{S3. Carbon footprint estimation and domain mapping}

Carbon footprints are calculated using a hybrid approach integrating transaction-level expenditures, category-specific emissions intensities, and targeted profile and registry refinements, following \citet{andersson2020novel}. Expenditures are mapped to footprint categories, harmonized monthly using consumer price index by category, and aggregated to daily and annual totals.

At the transaction level, emissions are obtained by multiplying expenditure by category-specific intensity factors (kg CO$_2$e/SEK). For selected domains where expenditure is an especially noisy proxy for physical quantities, registry and profile information is used to refine the mapping. In particular, transport and housing components are refined where relevant information is available, and food-related estimates are calibrated using diet information from the app's profile survey.

\paragraph{Aviation emission intensity override.}
The default input--output-based emission intensity for aviation is replaced with a bottom-up estimate that accounts for non-CO$_2$ climate effects (contrails, contrail-induced cirrus, NO$_x$-driven ozone formation, and water vapour). Aviation combustion emits approximately 0.08~kg~CO$_2$ per passenger-kilometre (pkm), consistent with the European fleet average of 83~g~CO$_2$/pkm reported for 2023 \citep{easa2025}. To convert expenditure to physical units, we use an average ticket yield of 0.82~SEK/pkm. This value is the RPK-share-weighted mean passenger revenue yield (ticket and ancillary revenue per revenue passenger-kilometre) calculated from the FY2024 annual reports of six major carriers serving the European market---Ryanair, Lufthansa Group, easyJet, Wizz Air, SAS, and Norwegian---weighted by each carrier's share of their combined revenue passenger-kilometres, with foreign-currency revenues converted at Riksbanken 2024 annual-average exchange rates. The corresponding value computed from FY2023 reports is 0.81~SEK/pkm, indicating that the estimate is stable over the study period. Non-CO$_2$ effects are incorporated via a multiplicative uplift factor of 1.5 applied to combustion CO$_2$, following the central estimate of \citet{lee2021contribution}. An additional 20\% of combustion CO$_2$ is added to account for upstream (well-to-tank) emissions from crude extraction, refining, and fuel transport, consistent with the conventional Jet~A life-cycle inventory of \citet{stratton2010lifecycle}. The resulting intensity is:
\[
  \frac{0.08 \times 1.5 + 0.08 \times 0.20}{0.82} \approx 0.166 \;\text{kg CO}_2\text{e/SEK}
\]
This override is applied to all aviation transactions before aggregation. The resulting intensity is approximately 42\% higher than the default environmentally-extended input--output (EE-IOA) value, which is itself a full life-cycle estimate but does not capture the non-CO$_2$ forcing components specific to high-altitude jet emissions. The adjustment therefore primarily affects the direct component of the flight-free contrast.

For each focal lifestyle contrast, emissions are decomposed into:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Direct emissions}: emissions in the focal domain.
  \item \textbf{Indirect emissions}: total minus direct emissions.
\end{itemize}

Direct-domain mapping follows the main analysis design:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Car-free}: the short-distance transport domain, comprising fuel, car maintenance, car rental, vehicle purchases, public transport, bus, taxi, e-scooter, and other local transport. This domain is defined broadly to include mode substitution within the transport system: if a car-free household uses public transit or car rental instead of driving their own car, that substitution is captured within the direct component rather than appearing as apparent indirect rebound. This ensures that the indirect component reflects genuine cross-domain differences rather than within-transport mode shifts.
  \item \textbf{Flight-free}: the aviation domain (a single transaction category). This is a deliberately narrow definition. Any substitution toward long-distance train travel cannot be clearly separated from local public transit in transaction data and is therefore assigned to the indirect component. This makes the indirect estimate for flight-free households conservative: train-for-plane substitution would increase indirect emissions, working against a finding of cross-domain coherence.
  \item \textbf{Meat-free}: the grocery domain only. Restaurant emissions are estimated as the service component only (not the food itself), to avoid double-counting with grocery-based food emissions. Alcohol is sold exclusively through the state monopoly (Systembolaget) and is a separate transaction category. Bar, café, and tobacco spending are similarly excluded from the direct domain. This narrow definition ensures that the direct component captures differences in food purchasing patterns attributable to diet, while other food-adjacent spending contributes to the indirect component.
\end{itemize}

\section*{S4. Survey measures and identity construction}

Environmental self-identity (ESI) is measured using three items adapted from \citet{VanDerWerff2013}: (i) ``Acting in an environmentally friendly way is an important part of who I am'', (ii) ``I am a person who acts in an environmentally friendly way'', and (iii) ``I perceive myself as an environmentally friendly person''. Responses are recorded on a 7-point Likert scale.

For descriptive purposes, we summarize ESI using the mean of the three items on the original scale. For regression analyses, ESI is operationalized as a standardized score (mean 0, SD 1). Internal consistency is high in the analytical sample (Cronbach's $\alpha = 0.88$).

Table~\ref{tab:esi_benchmark} compares the within-sample standardized ESI distribution to (i) an external Swedish reference sample using the same three-item instrument on a 5-point response scale \citep{eriksson2024integrating} and (ii) the green oversample (Section~S1). Z-scores are computed within each sample independently to maintain comparability across response scales. The analytical (random) sample has a slightly stronger right tail than the reference sample, consistent with mild positive selection on environmental orientation. Notably, the green oversample is \emph{not} more strongly self-identified as environmental than the random sample on this measure---if anything, it has marginally less mass at $z\geq 1$ (15.2\% vs 17.2\%). Voting Green is therefore not equivalent to scoring high on the ESI scale, and the green oversample is best treated as an independent verification sample rather than as a high-ESI subgroup (Section~S13).

\section*{S5. Variable construction}

Outcomes are 12-month total, direct, and indirect emissions. Lifestyle indicators are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{no\_car}: profile survey reports zero owned cars.
  \item \texttt{no\_flying}: zero aviation spending in the observation window.
  \item \texttt{no\_meat}: profile survey indicates vegetarian, pescetarian/vegfish, or vegan diet.
\end{itemize}

Control variables follow the main text: sex, age group, education, disposable income, children, housing type, major-city indicator, and log population density.

\section*{S6. Model specification and robustness checks}

\subsection*{S6.1 Baseline specification}
For each lifestyle indicator $B_i \in \{0,1\}$ (car-free, flight-free, meat-free) we estimate an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression of household $i$'s annualised emissions $E_i$:
\[
E_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 B_i + \beta_2 \mathit{ESI}_i + \beta_3 (B_i \times \mathit{ESI}_i) + \mathbf{X}_i\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_i .
\]
$\beta_1$ is the lifestyle main effect at mean ESI; $\beta_2$ is the ESI main effect among non-adopters; $\beta_3$ tests whether the lifestyle effect varies with ESI. $\mathbf{X}_i$ collects the controls listed in Section~S5. All standard errors are heteroskedasticity-robust of the HC3 form.

The lifestyle indicators retain adequate within-sample variation for estimating both main and interaction terms under the baseline specification.

\subsection*{S6.2 Sensitivity to uncategorized-share threshold}

The baseline analysis requires that uncategorized spending make up at most 10\% of total spending over the 12-month observation window. For each participant, we compute the rolling 12-month uncategorized share and select the \emph{most recent} 12-month window in which the share is at or below the 10\% threshold for the entire window. Each participant is therefore aligned to their latest high-quality 12-month period rather than to a fixed calendar window. Figure~\ref{fig:threshold_sensitivity} reports total and indirect lifestyle coefficients at thresholds of 5\%, 10\% (baseline), 15\%, and 20\%. Total effects are directionally stable across thresholds.

Sample size does not increase strictly as the threshold is loosened (e.g. the 15\% sample is marginally smaller than the 10\% sample). Changing the threshold can change which 12-month window is selected for a given participant, which in turn affects whether that participant has complete data on every control variable; participants missing any control are dropped, so a looser threshold does not always yield more usable observations.

\subsection*{S6.3 Alternative diet-group definitions}
Because ``meat-free'' pools several distinct diets, we test the sensitivity of the main result to how the group is defined, including a vegan-only subgroup. The vegan-only subgroup of the larger meat-free group shows particularly large and statistically significant differences in both direct and indirect emissions (Fig.~\ref{fig:diet_definitions}). The vegan-only subgroup ($n=21$) shows large negative direct and indirect estimates, consistent with prior Swedish evidence \citep{AnderssonNassen2023}, though the small sample and the narrow, single-item diet measure mean both should be read as directional rather than precise.

\subsection*{S6.4 Temporal-window robustness}
Low indirect emissions during the observation window may reflect deferred, rather than foregone, consumption. We address this by re-estimating the main models using all available post-COVID months per participant (mean = 21, range 12--39) annualised, rather than the baseline 12-month window. If savings are merely deferred, indirect effects should weaken toward zero under the longer window. Table~\ref{tab:temporal_extended} compares the baseline 12-month and extended-window indirect lifestyle coefficients.

For car-free households, the indirect coefficient is essentially unchanged ($-$2\%). The meat-free indirect estimate, which was near zero and not significant at baseline ($-$10\,kg, $p = 0.97$), changes sign in the extended window, indicating that the 12-month point estimate was largely noise. Neither pattern is consistent with deferred consumption, which would predict systematic attenuation toward zero from a clearly negative baseline.

For flight-free households, the indirect estimate strengthens ($+$26\%). This most likely reflects a composition effect rather than new spillover: the set of households that qualify as flight-free shrinks under the longer window, because some households that recorded no flights within their 12-month window did fly within the wider window. These households---occasional rather than committed non-flyers---were nonetheless counted as flight-free in the baseline analysis. Restricting attention to households that remain flight-free over the longer horizon (zero aviation spending across the full window, mean 21 months) strengthens the indirect coefficient to $-$463\,kg CO$_2$e/year ($p = 0.027$), i.e.\ it becomes individually significant under the stricter, internally consistent definition in which both group membership and emissions are measured over the same extended window. This is consistent with our main interpretation---genuine cross-domain restraint among committed non-flyers---rather than with deferred consumption.

\subsection*{S9.1 Equivalence tests against the re-spending benchmark}

A non-significant indirect coefficient indicates only that a zero effect cannot be rejected; it does not establish that rebound is absent. To address this directly, we apply two one-sided tests (TOST) of statistical equivalence. For each lifestyle we take the headline (additive) indirect-emissions estimate $\hat\beta$ and its HC3 standard error, and specify an equivalence margin $\Delta$ equal to the proportional re-spending benchmark for that lifestyle (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_rebound}). Because $\Delta$ is the indirect-emissions increase a standard proportional re-spending model predicts, it is the policy-relevant rebound magnitude. The two one-sided tests evaluate $H_0^{-}:\beta \le -\Delta$ and $H_0^{+}:\beta \ge +\Delta$; rejecting both (i.e.\ the larger of the two $p$-values falls below $0.05$) implies the indirect effect lies within $\pm\Delta$ of zero and is thus statistically equivalent to no economically meaningful rebound. We additionally report the one-sided rebound-exclusion test $H_0:\beta \ge +\Delta$, which asks whether the data rule out a rebound as large as the benchmark, together with the 90\% confidence interval matched to the $\alpha=0.05$ one-sided tests. These quantities are produced by \texttt{81\_equivalence\_income\_tests.R} and exported to \texttt{equivalence\_tests.csv}.

For car-free and flight-free households, the one-sided test rejects a rebound as large as the proportional re-spending benchmark ($p=0.041$ and $p=0.045$, respectively): the data are inconsistent with indirect emissions rising to the benchmark level implied by standard re-spending assumptions. The symmetric TOST is not significant because the indirect point estimates lie near the lower (reduction) margin rather than near zero---these effects are reductions, not nulls---so equivalence to a null within $\pm\Delta$ cannot be claimed. For meat-free households the benchmark margin is very small ($\pm$8~kg, reflecting negligible focal-domain savings), so neither test is informative. A caveat applies to all of these tests: the margin $\Delta$ is treated as a fixed quantity, although it is constructed from an estimated spending coefficient with its own sampling error, which is not propagated into the test statistics; the rebound-exclusion $p$-values should therefore be read as conditional on the benchmark point estimate.

\section*{S10. Inverse-probability weighting robustness check}

We report two complementary inverse-probability weighting (IPW) checks. IPW reweights the sample so that under-represented groups count for more and over-represented groups for less, approximating the estimate that would be obtained from a more balanced or representative sample. The first re-weights for selection on observed characteristics (observables) \emph{into} lifestyle groups (treatment IPW). The second re-weights for selection \emph{into the analytical sample} from the invited population frame (sampling IPW), addressing concerns that environmentally oriented or otherwise non-representative individuals may be over-represented among consenting participants.

\paragraph{Treatment IPW.}
Propensity scores are estimated via logistic regression of each lifestyle indicator on the full set of control variables. Figure~\ref{fig:robustness_comparison} compares IPW-weighted and green-sample (Section~S13) total-outcome coefficients alongside the unweighted baseline. The core findings are robust: large, significant lifestyle main effects for car-free and flight-free households, with magnitudes comparable to the unweighted estimates. The meat-free total effect remains negative and reaches significance under treatment IPW ($-$608~kg, $p = 0.037$), while remaining marginal in the unweighted models (Table~\ref{tab:sampling_ipw}). ESI~$\times$~lifestyle interactions on total emissions are non-significant in all cases, consistent with the baseline models.

\paragraph{Sampling IPW.}
To assess whether the lifestyle main effects are robust to differential participation across the invited frame, we construct cell-based sampling weights using the cross-classification of sex, age group (18--29, 30--44, 45--65), and major-city status. Cell-specific raw participation rates range from approximately 1\% to 3\% (Table~\ref{tab:ipw_cells}). Each respondent is assigned the inverse of their cell's participation probability and the weights are normalized to the analytical sample size. We then re-estimate the main interaction models with these weights.

Across all three lifestyle main effects (total, direct, and indirect outcomes), the substantive conclusions are unchanged under sampling-weighted estimation (Table~\ref{tab:sampling_ipw}). The large car-free and flight-free total and direct effects change by less than 5\% and remain highly significant; the meat-free total effect remains negative and non-significant under both unweighted and weighted estimation. ESI main effects remain negative and similarly significant under both weighting schemes. The only sign change among the lifestyle main effects is the small meat-free indirect coefficient ($-$10 vs $+$42~kg), which is statistically indistinguishable from zero in both versions ($p\geq0.88$) and economically negligible; sign reversals likewise occur only for ESI~$\times$~lifestyle interaction terms that are far from significance in either model, and so do not change inference.

\section*{S12. Composition of indirect differences across broad consumption domains}

The category-level decomposition (Figure~\ref{fig:cat_decomp}) shows where indirect emission differences come from across non-focal consumption domains, and how these patterns differ by environmental self-identity (ESI). For each lifestyle contrast, the focal domain is excluded (car \& public transport for car-free, aviation for flight-free, food for meat-free households), and category-level estimates are evaluated at $+1$~SD (high ESI) and $-1$~SD (low ESI).

Two regularities stand out. First, at high ESI the indirect reductions are broadly distributed: many non-focal domains contribute small to moderate negative differences, with no single category accounting for the bulk of the effect. This pattern is consistent with general consumption restraint among environmentally identified adopters, and it is most pronounced in the categories that are themselves emission-intensive (food, vacation \& long-distance travel, other products). Second, at low ESI the residual differences concentrate in specific substitution channels that are intuitively linked to the foregone behaviour: car-free low-ESI households show a clear positive estimate in aviation \& long-distance travel ($+$0.24~tCO$_2$e), suggesting substitution toward flying; flight-free low-ESI households show a positive estimate in car \& public transport ($+$0.27). Meat-free low-ESI households show a positive estimate in other products ($+$0.35) and a smaller positive estimate in car \& public transport ($+$0.19).

For car-free households at high ESI, reductions are spread across other products ($-$0.23), food ($-$0.22), and other/misc ($-$0.16), with smaller negative contributions from vacation \& LDT, other services, and housing. For flight-free households at high ESI, reductions span food ($-$0.18), vacation \& LDT ($-$0.15), other/misc ($-$0.14), and other products ($-$0.10). For meat-free households at high ESI, the directionally largest reductions are in aviation \& LDT ($-$0.13) and car \& public transport ($-$0.10), although the aggregate high-ESI indirect effect for this contrast is not statistically significant ($p=0.23$).

\section*{S13. Green-sample replication robustness}
The green oversample (Section~S1) is recruited from electoral districts with high Green Party vote shares but, as Table~\ref{tab:esi_benchmark} shows, it does \emph{not} score higher than the random sample on the ESI scale. Voting Green and self-identifying as environmental on the three-item instrument therefore measure different things, and the green oversample should not be interpreted as a high-ESI subgroup. We use it instead as an independent verification sample: a different recruitment frame and a different community context, but the same data pipeline.

Figure~\ref{fig:robustness_comparison} compares the green-sample lifestyle main effects on total emissions with the random-sample baseline and the IPW estimates. All three contrasts replicate in direction and significance: car-free $-$1{,}507~kg, $p<0.001$; flight-free $-$2{,}064~kg, $p<0.001$; meat-free $-$640~kg, $p<0.001$. The negative indirect effects are consistently stronger and also reach conventional significance in this larger sample for car-free and flight-free households (and approach it for meat-free). As with the total effects, we treat this as external corroboration of direction, given the non-random recruitment. ESI~$\times$~lifestyle interactions on total emissions are non-significant in the green sample, consistent with the random-sample finding that interaction terms on total emissions are typically small.

\section*{S14. Recategorized-transactions robustness}
To verify that the results do not depend on the transaction recategorization rules applied in the main pipeline, we re-estimated the main interaction models on an alternative version of the transaction data that omits those rules (the \texttt{full\_data\_norecats\_filters\_260121.dta} input), holding the sample and all controls fixed ($N=1{,}297$).
