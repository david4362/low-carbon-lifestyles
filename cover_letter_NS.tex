Dear Editors,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, ``Low-carbon lifestyles show substantially lower emissions with no sign of rebound,'' for consideration as an
Article in \textit{Nature Sustainability}.

How much can lifestyle change contribute to climate mitigation? The most recent global assessment puts the direct potential at some 10~GtCO$_2$e per year among high-consuming households, but since the money saved is assumed to be re-spent on other consumption, typically offsetting around half of the mitigation. This re-spending assumption, inherited from energy-efficiency analysis, is embedded in the models that quantify demand-side mitigation, yet it has never been tested against the actual spending of people who live low-carbon lives. We provide that test.

Linking twelve months of bank-transaction records---covering
close to the full consumption budget---with administrative registers and survey responses for a random sample of 1,297 Swedish single-adult
households, we observe, rather than simulate, the complete spending and emission patterns of established car-free, flight-free, and meat-free households, comparing them with non-adopters while holding income, education, age, household composition, and residential context constant.

We believe three findings will interest your readership. First, lifestyle adopters show large net emission reductions---adjusted differences of $-$25\% and $-$26\% for car-free and flight-free living relative to comparison-group footprints. Second, there is no automatic rebound: despite large financial savings, adopters emit \textit{less}, not more, across the rest of their budget. Third, motivation shapes the outcome: households with stronger environmental self-identity show lower emissions across the entire budget, so the net climate benefit of a lifestyle transition depends on who adopts it, and why. 

The measurement approach itself may also interest your readers. Although rebound is central to how demand-side mitigation is estimated, no prior study has observed what low-carbon adopters actually do with the money they save: spillover research tracks behaviours rather than spending, and existing rebound estimates are simulated or rely on self-reported expenditure. Population-scale linkage of financial transactions, administrative registers, and psychological constructs opens a new path to analyse and understand everyday consumption behaviour directly.

The work is original and not under consideration elsewhere. We declare one competing interest, detailed in the manuscript: David Andersson is also the founder of Svalna AB, which developed the mobile app and data-collection infrastructure used in the study (and has since ceased consumer-facing operations); statistical analysis, interpretation of results, and manuscript preparation were conducted independently of Svalna AB.
