\title[Low-carbon lifestyles deliver broad climate benefits]{Low-carbon lifestyles deliver broad emission reductions without automatic rebound}

\abstract{A central concern in demand-side mitigation is that low-carbon lifestyle choices may free financial resources that are redirected toward spending and emissions elsewhere. We combine high-resolution financial transaction data with registry records and survey responses for 1,311 Swedish single-adult households. For three lifestyle contrasts---living without a car, avoiding air travel, and following a meat-free diet---we estimate total emission differences and decompose them into direct (within-domain) and indirect (all other consumption) effects. Controlling for income and demographic, household, and location characteristics, we find that mobility-related lifestyles are associated with substantially lower total emissions ($-$1.51 and $-$2.06 tCO$_2$e per person-year, respectively; both p~< 0.001), while a meat-free diet shows directionally lower total emissions ($-$0.47 tCO$_2$e; p~= 0.084). We find no evidence that these lifestyle contrasts are associated with higher indirect effects: differences are $-$0.25 tCO$_2$e for car-free households (p~= 0.39), $-$0.21 tCO$_2$e for flight-free households (p~= 0.32), and $-$0.01 tCO$_2$e for meat-free households (p~= 0.98), despite large financial savings in the first two. Environmental self-identity (ESI) stratifies indirect emission patterns: stronger ESI is associated with consistently lower non-focal emissions (significant in flight and diet models, p~= 0.035 and 0.003; marginal in the car model, p~= 0.061). High-ESI adopters show broad reductions across non-focal consumption ($-$0.64 and $-$0.65 tCO$_2$e for car-free and flight-free households, relative to an ESI-neutral non-adopter reference), while low-ESI adopters sit at or slightly above the comparison level. Overall, results challenge automatic rebound assumptions and indicate that environmental motivation shapes how redirected spending maps onto indirect emissions.}

\keywords{rebound effect, carbon footprint, environmental self-identity, demand-side mitigation, household consumption}

\maketitle

Limiting global warming to well below 2~$^\circ$C requires deep and sustained reductions in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions across all sectors \citep{IPCC_AR6_WG3_2022}. Demand-side change is increasingly recognized as a major mitigation opportunity \citep{Creutzig2022}. Among household actions in high-income contexts, reduced car dependence, avoided air travel, and plant-based diets are consistently identified as high-impact options \citep{Wynes2017}.

A central question is whether direct emissions reductions in one domain are offset by higher emissions elsewhere---the rebound effect. Financial savings from reduced consumption in one domain might be substituted toward other emitting goods, potentially eroding the climate benefit \citep{Berkhout2000,SorrellDimitropoulos2008,Hertwich2005,sorrell2020,Reimers2022}. Energy and emissions modeling frameworks routinely approximate these indirect effects using proportional or marginal re-spending calibrated on household expenditure patterns \citep{Chitnis2014,Bjelle2018}, effectively treating freed resources as reallocating mechanically across a representative consumption bundle. Such rebound assumptions can substantially reduce the estimated climate benefits of lifestyle change in mitigation scenarios. 

People avoid cars, air traveling, and meat for many reasons, including urban living, fear of flying, health, and animal welfare concerns. If a household avoids car ownership or air travel for environmental reasons, that same motivation may also discourage substitution toward other carbon-intensive alternatives. Identity theory provides a framework for this: individuals with strong environmental self-identity (ESI)---who see pro-environmental action as part of who they are---may behave more consistently across domains \citep{VanDerWerff2013,WhitmarshONeill2010,udall2021}. Such positive spillover has been documented in survey and experimental studies \citep{Truelove2014,thogersen1999spillover}, but so has negative spillover where ``doing good'' in one domain licenses people to ``doing bad'' in another \citep{Merritt2010,Blanken2015}. Reviews conclude that the direction and magnitude of such effects depend on context, identity salience, and behaviour type, and that evidence from real-world comprehensive data is still limited \citep{Nilsson2017}.

Empirical tests of indirect relationships are rare because high-resolution data across all behavioural domains are seldom available for population-based samples \citep{exadaktylos2021energy}, although recent advances in footprinting from bank-transaction records make such tests feasible at the household level \citep{trendl2023}. In this study, we test whether individuals with three low-carbon lifestyles—living car-free, avoiding air travel, and following a meat-free diet—have higher emissions outside the focal domain: a pattern expected under common re-spending assumptions, whereby resources freed up in the focal domain are reallocated proportionally across other consumption. To do this, we link comprehensive financial transaction data, registry records, and survey responses for a random sample of 1{,}311 single-adult Swedish households. For each lifestyle choice, we estimate 12-month emission differences---controlling for income, demographic, household, and location characteristics---and decompose them into focal-domain (direct) and non-focal (indirect) components.

We find that all three lifestyle groups have lower total emissions and no group has higher emissions outside the focal domain, inconsistent with common assumptions that low-carbon lifestyle choices are partly offset by higher emissions elsewhere in the household consumption bundle. We then ask whether this pattern varies with environmental self-identity (ESI): first, whether indirect emission differences are more favorable among households with stronger ESI, and second, whether the lifestyle-specific gap itself widens with ESI—a stricter test, since high-ESI households may simply emit less regardless of lifestyle. We find the former but not the latter: high-ESI car-free and flight-free households have significantly lower indirect emissions, but there is no clear evidence that the gap between adopters and non-adopters widens with ESI.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Sample characteristics}
The analytical sample comprised 1{,}311 single-adult households meeting data quality criteria (Methods). Registry and survey descriptive summaries are reported in Supplementary Information, Section~S2. The mean annual footprint was estimated to approximately 5.37~tCO$_2$e per person. For context, official consumption-based statistics attribute close to 5~tCO$_2$e per person-year to Swedish household consumption \citep{naturvardsverket_konsumtion_2025}. The two figures are not directly comparable: our sample consists of single-adult households, which lack the household economies of scale that lower per-capita footprints in the general population, and our aviation intensity incorporates non-CO$_2$ high-altitude effects that the national model omits (Supplementary Information, Section~S3). Both differences are expected to place our estimate somewhat above the population-wide household average, consistent with what we observe.

\subsection{Average direct and indirect differences}
Living without a car is associated with a regression-adjusted total emissions difference of $-$1.51~tCO$_2$e per person-year ($p < 0.001$; Fig.~\ref{fig_main}). Avoiding air travel is associated with a total difference of $-$2.06~tCO$_2$e ($p < 0.001$). Following a meat-free diet is associated with a total difference of $-$0.47~tCO$_2$e that is directionally consistent but does not reach significance in this sample ($p = 0.084$).

For car-free households, the direct component is $-$1.26~tCO$_2$e and the indirect component is $-$0.25~tCO$_2$e ($p = 0.39$). For flight-free households, the direct component is $-$1.84~tCO$_2$e and the indirect component is $-$0.21~tCO$_2$e ($p = 0.32$). For meat-free households, the direct component is $-$0.47~tCO$_2$e ($p < 0.001$), while the indirect component is small and non-significant ($-$0.01~tCO$_2$e, $p = 0.98$). Across the two mobility-related lifestyles, indirect effects are negative (in the spillover direction), though not individually significant at conventional levels. Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests confirm that the indirect pattern reflects a broad-based difference in non-focal consumption rather than the influence of outliers: the full distribution of indirect emissions is shifted toward lower values for car-free ($p = 0.027$) and flight-free households ($p = 0.007$), while no strictly significant distributional difference is found for meat-free households ($p = 0.060$; Fig.~\ref{fig_violin}). 

\subsection{Proportional re-spending benchmark}
As a stylized benchmark, we approximate indirect emissions under proportional re-spending: the regression-adjusted reduction in focal-domain spending is multiplied by the mean outside-domain emissions intensity of the comparison group (Methods). 

For car-free households, the benchmark predicts an indirect increase of $+$0.256~tCO$_2$e from proportional reallocation of freed resources, whereas the observed indirect estimate is $-$0.255~tCO$_2$e, a gap of $-$0.511~tCO$_2$e. For flight-free households, the benchmark predicts $+$0.151~tCO$_2$e, against an observed $-$0.212~tCO$_2$e, a gap of $-$0.363~tCO$_2$e. For meat-free households, the benchmark predicts $-$0.008~tCO$_2$e (because focal-domain spending is higher rather than lower), while the observed indirect effect is $-$0.005~tCO$_2$e. For car-free and flight-free households, one-sided tests reject a rebound as large as the proportional re-spending benchmark ($p = 0.041$ and $p = 0.045$; Supplementary Information, Section~S9.1), so the observed shortfall relative to the benchmark is statistically robust rather than merely descriptive.

\subsection{The role of environmental motivation in indirect emission patterns}
The average indirect differences above pool households across the full range of environmental motivation. We therefore run two complementary ESI analyses. The first asks how the redirected-spending \emph{position} depends on environmental motivation, by evaluating predicted indirect emissions at low ($-$1~SD), mean, and high ($+$1~SD) ESI relative to a common ESI-neutral (ESI~$=0$) non-adopter reference (Fig.~\ref{fig_esi}). This gradient is governed by the ESI main effect on non-focal emissions, which is sizable and statistically detectable. Each standard-deviation increase in ESI is associated with lower indirect emissions in all three models ($p = 0.061$, $0.035$, and $0.003$ for the car, flight, and diet models, respectively). Consequently, high-ESI adopters occupy broadly lower-emission positions than the proportional re-spending benchmark implies ($-$0.64~tCO$_2$e for car-free and $-$0.65~tCO$_2$e for flight-free households relative to the ESI-neutral reference), whereas low-ESI adopters sit near or slightly above it ($+$0.12 and $+$0.21~tCO$_2$e, respectively). While high-ESI adopters have lower indirect emissions, the ESI × adoption interaction is not significant. The pattern thus reflects broad environmental identification predicting cautious spending, rather than adoption itself driving stronger behavioral restraint at high ESI.

A category-level decomposition reinforces this allocation reading. Relative to the same ESI-neutral reference, high-ESI adopters show reductions distributed broadly and coherently across most non-focal categories. Low-ESI adopters' positions are more mixed, and any higher emissions concentrate in a few plausible substitution channels---notably other high-carbon focal domains, with elevated aviation emissions among car-free households and elevated car and public transport emissions among flight-free households (Fig.~\ref{fig_category}; Supplementary Information, Section~S12). Supplementary Information, Section~S7 visualises these patterns as continuous predicted emission levels across the full ESI gradient.

The second analysis is a stricter test of whether environmental motivation \emph{amplifies} the adoption effect itself---what we term principled spillover, an identity-consistency mechanism in the sense of \citep{VanDerWerff2013,Truelove2014}. The question is whether the indirect-emission gap between adopters and non-adopters widens as ESI rises, over and above the general tendency for more environmentally identified people to emit less. Statistically this is the ESI~$\times$~lifestyle interaction: under purely mechanical rebound it should be zero, under principled spillover it should be negative, with adopters pulling further ahead at higher ESI. We do not find this. The interaction terms are not significant for any lifestyle, on either indirect CO$_2$e ($p = 0.60$, $0.42$, and $0.97$ for the car, flight, and diet models) or indirect spending (Supplementary Information, Section~S7), so the high- versus low-ESI gradient described above is attributable to the ESI main effect on allocation rather than to motivation-specific amplification of adoption.

The interaction estimates are nonetheless directionally suggestive for the two mobility lifestyles: the adopter slope against ESI is slightly steeper than the non-adopter slope (interaction coefficients $-$99 and $-$129~kg\,CO$_2$e per SD), the pattern principled spillover would predict. The effect is, however, small and statistically indistinguishable from zero. This is also what one would expect even if such a mechanism were real: an interaction is a second-order effect, and detecting one reliably typically requires several times the sample needed to identify the corresponding main effect, so a single population sample of this size is unlikely to resolve it. Establishing principled spillover would require clear, robust negative ESI~$\times$~lifestyle interactions; that criterion is not met here, and we therefore rest our ESI claims on the well-identified main-effect (allocation) pattern rather than on motivation-specific moderation.

\section{Discussion}
Economic rebound theory predicts that savings from one domain are mechanically reallocated across other goods and services \citep{Berkhout2000,sorrell2020}. The central finding of this work is that we do not observe re-spending in this random-sample setting. For car-free and flight-free households, indirect emissions are lower despite substantial financial savings, and benchmark comparisons suggest that proportional re-spending assumptions overstate offsetting. Our analysis points to one plausible source of the more favorable indirect pattern: environmental self-identity is strongly associated with how the remaining budget is allocated, so that adopters with stronger ESI occupy broadly lower-emission positions outside the focal domain. Low-ESI adopters do not exhibit the broad positive rebound that proportional re-spending implies, although category-level patterns indicate relatively higher spending in some climate-intensive substitution channels (Fig.~4). Re-spending, where it occurs, is thus selective rather than proportional: no adopter group reallocates freed resources mechanically across the consumption bundle, suggesting that lifestyle adoption entails different consumption practices rather than uniform restraint or uniform reallocation.

Our principled spillover test provides the stricter quasi-causal check, and it is not met: the ESI $\times$ lifestyle interactions on indirect emissions and spending are not significant, so principled spillover is not established, though a larger sample may be needed to detect a second-order effect of this kind. The consistently negative ESI main effect on indirect emissions nonetheless indicates that environmental motivation is associated with lower non-focal consumption in general, which remains relevant for how aggregate re-spending should be represented.

More broadly, our results are consistent with our previous findings of no rebound in a self-selected environmentally motivated sample \citep{AnderssonNassen2023} and lack of direct rebound for households who changed to a ``green car'' as opposed to others who changed to a non-labelled fuel-efficient car \citep{andersson2019estimating}. The present study extends that evidence by showing that the favourable indirect pattern is most pronounced at higher levels of environmental self-identity within a population-based sample; a robustness check re-estimating the interaction model on the non-random ``green'' subsample confirms that lifestyle main effects remain strongly negative (Supplementary Information, Section~S13).

The comparison between observed indirect differences and the proportional re-spending benchmark informs how rebound is represented in consumption-based emissions modelling. For both car-free and flight-free households, observed indirect emissions fall substantially below benchmark predictions (gaps of $-$0.51 and $-$0.36~tCO$_2$e respectively), suggesting that stylised proportional reallocation assumptions can overstate offsetting in mobility-related domains \citep{Bjelle2018,Chitnis2014,grabs2015rebound}. Distributional tests reinforce this, with significant left-shifts in residualized indirect emissions for car-free and flight-free households (Supplementary Information, Section~S8). For meat-free households, the benchmark and observed indirect values are both close to zero, consistent with the smaller financial savings involved.

These findings matter for quantitative modelling of demand-side mitigation. Many models impose budget closure with representative-agent demand systems, so savings in one category are mechanically redistributed according to average substitution elasticities \citep{Chitnis2013,Deaton1980,Gillingham2016,Niamir2020}. Our results suggest both that the re-spending assumption may be erroneous, and that averaging over motivation may misstate net indirect effects: households with stronger environmental motivation show systematically lower non-focal emissions.

Several limitations should be noted. Because the analysis is observational and cross-sectional, all estimates reflect population-based associations and should not be interpreted as causal effects of adopting a behaviour. Individuals who live without a car, avoid air travel, or follow meat-free diets likely differ from their comparison groups on dimensions not fully captured by observed controls, including preferences and built-environment access beyond population density and urbanity. Similarly, environmental self-identity is self-reported and measured contemporaneously with consumption; identity and behaviour plausibly reinforce one another over time, so the ESI gradients should be read as associations rather than one-directional influences. The restriction to single-adult households simplifies the attribution of spending to a single consumption profile but limits generalizability.

At the same time, the cross-sectional design has a compensating strength for the spillover question. Negative spillover and moral licensing are documented largely in experimental and short-run settings where a recently adopted behaviour is salient \citep{Merritt2010,Blanken2015}; if compensatory responses are most pronounced during behavioural transitions, our design---observing established rather than newly induced behaviours---captures the settled phase most relevant for long-run mitigation, a conjecture that longitudinal designs could test directly.

The Swedish cultural context deserves particular attention. Sweden combines comparatively high environmental concern and carbon literacy with a long-standing public discourse emphasising individual responsibility for one's carbon footprint \citep{eib_climate_2021,naturvardsverket_2023,oecd_sweden_2025}---reflected in the emergence of \textit{flygskam} (``flight shame''), which originated in Sweden in 2018 \citep{wolrath2019grounded}. These norms may reinforce the behavioural consistency associated with environmental self-identity, making the ESI gradient observed here partly context-dependent. To the extent that environmental self-identity is shaped by social discourse, past behaviour, and norm formation rather than fixed individual disposition \citep{vanderwerff2014past,nyborg2016social}, the motivational composition of a population is itself a potential object of policy. Our cross-sectional design cannot test this dynamic, but it implies that the aggregate indirect emissions accompanying lifestyle change may depend on the normative context in which adoption occurs.

Measurement limitations of expenditure-based footprinting---imperfect transaction categorisation and the inability to distinguish within-category intensity variation---are detailed in Methods; both work against detecting the indirect reductions we report, making our estimates conservative. Likewise, a single 12-month window pools incidental with committed non-flyers, biasing the flight-free estimate toward zero. Under a longer confirmatory window requiring zero air travel throughout, the flight-free indirect reduction strengthens to $-$0.46~tCO$_2$e ($p = 0.027$), supporting genuine restraint over deferred consumption; car-free and meat-free results are stable (Methods; Supplementary Information, Section~S6.4).

Despite these limitations, the results provide rare empirical evidence that low-carbon lifestyles are not systematically offset by indirect emission increases in a population-based sample. We find no evidence of automatic rebound for car-free or flight-free households: indirect emissions are lower, not higher, relative to demographically similar comparison households, and the observed differences fall well below proportional re-spending benchmarks. This challenges a common assumption in demand-side mitigation modelling. Beyond the main no-rebound finding, the more favorable indirect pattern at higher ESI suggests that motivational composition is relevant to how aggregate indirect emissions should be represented. If lifestyle transitions occur at scale, the net climate benefit will depend not only on the direct emissions avoided but also on whether adoption is driven by environmental concern or cost savings alone. Future research should examine whether similar no-rebound patterns hold in multi-person households, whether they extend to less binary adoption decisions such as vehicle replacement, and whether cross-national comparisons across contexts with different environmental norms can identify how cultural and social context shapes direct and, in particular, indirect emission effects in different groups.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Data and sample}

We link survey responses, official registry data, and 12 months of bank transaction records for a random sample of 1{,}311 Swedish adults. Consenting participants installed a secure app to share pseudonymised transaction data from their bank(s). The analysis is restricted to single-adult households to align transaction-recorded spending and survey-reported identity with a single consumption profile. Participants with more than 10\% uncategorised spending or incomplete 12-month time windows are excluded. Registry data provided demographic information (sex, age, income, and household composition), and surveys included environmental self-identity. Details on recruitment, ethics, sample construction, and benchmarking against the invited sampling frame are provided in Supplementary Information Sections S1--S2.

The primary analysis uses a fixed 12-month observation window. A single year may not fully capture inter-annual variability in infrequent behaviours such as air travel: it pools incidental with committed non-flyers, which biases the flight-free estimate (defined as no aviation spending over 12 months) toward zero. We therefore complement the primary 12-month analysis with a confirmatory longer-window specification (mean 21 months, range 12--39) that requires zero air travel across the full observation period (Supplementary Information, Section~S6.4).

\subsection{Carbon footprint estimation}
Transactions are mapped to consumption categories and linked to emission intensities derived from a Swedish environmentally extended input–output model \citep{andersson2020novel}. Annual emissions are computed as the sum of daily emission aggregates. Direct emissions are defined within the focal domain; indirect emissions are defined as total minus direct. Carbon footprint estimation and the mapping of behaviours to direct domains are described in Supplementary Information Section S3.

Transaction records offer near-complete coverage of consumption, but automatic categorisation of purchases is imperfect: direct transfers and purchases at small or unusual retailers cannot always be classified. We therefore require at least 90\% of each household's spending over the observation window to be successfully categorised, and sensitivity analyses confirm that lifestyle coefficients are stable across thresholds of 5--20\% uncategorised spending (Supplementary Information, Section~S6.2). As with all expenditure-based footprinting, carbon intensities are derived from broad input--output categories and cannot distinguish within-category variation, for instance between premium and budget products \citep{deHaan}. To the extent that environmentally motivated households select more expensive, lower-emission products within a category, this measurement limitation works against detecting the indirect reductions we report, making our estimates conservative.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
We estimate linear models of 12-month emissions as a function of each lifestyle indicator. For each lifestyle contrast $j \in \{\text{car-free, flight-free, meat-free}\}$ and each outcome $Y_i$ (total, direct, and indirect emissions; analogously for spending), the headline estimates come from the additive linear model
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive}
Y_i = \alpha + \beta_j L_{ij} + \mathbf{X}_i^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $L_{ij}$ indicates adoption of lifestyle $j$ and $\mathbf{X}_i$ contains income, sex, age group, education, children, housing type, a major-city indicator, and log population density. Models are estimated by ordinary least squares with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. We prefer this specification because the coefficients are adjusted mean differences in natural units (kg~CO$_2$e or SEK), and because total emissions equal the sum of direct and indirect emissions, estimating Eq.~\eqref{eq:additive} for each component with identical covariates guarantees that the direct and indirect coefficients sum exactly to the total coefficient---the additive decomposition shown in Fig.~\ref{fig_main}. Regression models exclude 15 households whose credit-card transfers exceed 30\% of total spending, because their downstream spending cannot be attributed to consumption domains, yielding a regression sample of 1{,}296 households (Supplementary Information, Section~S2).

The ESI analyses augment Eq.~\eqref{eq:additive} with standardized environmental self-identity and its interaction with adoption:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interaction}
Y_i = \alpha + \beta_j L_{ij} + \delta\,\mathrm{ESI}_i + \lambda_j \left(L_{ij} \times \mathrm{ESI}_i\right) + \mathbf{X}_i^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_i.
\end{equation}
Because ESI is standardized to mean zero and unit variance, $\beta_j$ retains its interpretation as the adoption difference at average ESI, $\delta$ captures the ESI gradient common to adopters and non-adopters, and $\lambda_j$ tests whether the adoption gap itself widens with ESI (principled spillover). From Eq.~\eqref{eq:interaction} we (i) evaluate predicted indirect emissions at low ($-$1~SD), mean, and high ($+$1~SD) ESI relative to a common ESI-neutral non-adopter reference (the ESI-conditioned allocation profile in Fig.~\ref{fig_esi}) and (ii) test $\lambda_j$ directly. All headline results and the re-spending benchmark below are based on Eq.~\eqref{eq:additive}; all ESI-conditioned quantities are based on Eq.~\eqref{eq:interaction}.

To assess the prevalence of offsetting patterns, indirect emissions are residualized on covariates and their distribution examined by group. 

Inverse-probability weights---both for selection on observables into lifestyle groups and for differential participation across the invited sampling frame---are used as robustness checks; weighted estimates are similar to unweighted results (Supplementary Information, Section~S10).

\subsection{Proportional re-spending benchmark}
For each lifestyle contrast $j$, we first estimate the regression-adjusted financial saving in the focal domain, $\Delta S_j$, by applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:additive} to focal-domain spending and reversing the sign of the adoption coefficient; $\Delta S_j$ is thus the mean saving attributable to the behaviour after conditioning on observed covariates. The benchmark indirect emission response is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:benchmark}
B_j = \Delta S_j \cdot \bar{\iota}_{-j}, \qquad \bar{\iota}_{-j} = \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{C}_j} E_{-j,i}}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{C}_j} S_{-j,i}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}_j$ is the comparison group of non-adopters and $E_{-j,i}$ and $S_{-j,i}$ denote household $i$'s emissions and spending outside the focal domain, so that $\bar{\iota}_{-j}$ is the comparison group's mean non-focal emission intensity (kg~CO$_2$e per SEK). $B_j$ approximates the indirect emission increase expected if freed financial resources were reallocated proportionally across the comparison group's non-focal consumption bundle. Because it assumes full re-spending at average intensity, the benchmark represents an upper-bound estimate under reallocation assumptions standard in modelling applications \citep{Bjelle2018,Chitnis2014,grabs2015rebound}; one-sided tests of the observed indirect estimates against $B_j$ are reported in Supplementary Information, Section~S9.1.
