\title{Low-carbon lifestyles deliver broad emission reductions without automatic rebound}
\author{David Andersson\thanks{Chalmers Next Labs; e-mail: david.andersson@chalmers.se}\and Jakob Enlund\thanks{Department of Economics and Statistics, Linnaeus University; e-mail: jakob.enlund@lnu.se} \and Jonas Nässén\thanks{Department of Environmental and Energy Sciences, Physical Resource Theory, Chalmers University of Technology; e-mail: jonas.nassen@chalmers.se}}

\begin{abstract}
Low-carbon lifestyle choices can free financial resources that lead to spending and emissions elsewhere. We combine high-resolution financial transaction data with registry records and survey responses for 1{,}311 randomly sampled Swedish single-adult households to estimate total, direct, and indirect emission differences associated with living without a car, avoiding air travel, and following a non-meat diet. Living without a car and avoiding air travel are associated with large total emission reductions ($-$1.43 and $-$2.11~tCO$_2$e per person-year; both $p < 0.001$). We find no evidence that savings are offset by higher emissions elsewhere: indirect emissions outside the focal domain are not higher for car-free ($-$0.18~tCO$_2$e, $p = 0.41$) or non-flying households ($-$0.29~tCO$_2$e, $p = 0.072$), despite large financial savings. In a second step, we examine the effects of environmental self-identity (ESI). At high ESI ($+$1~SD), indirect reductions are statistically significant for car-free and non-flying households; at low ESI ($-$1~SD) they are small and non-significant. Overall, the results indicate that rebound is not automatic and that environmental motivation is associated with more favorable indirect emission patterns.
\end{abstract}

Limiting global warming to well below 2~$^\circ$C requires deep and sustained reductions in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions across all sectors \citep{IPCC_AR6_WG3_2022}. Demand-side change is increasingly recognized as a major mitigation opportunity \citep{Creutzig2022}. Among household actions in high-income contexts, reduced car dependence, avoided air travel, and plant-based diets are consistently identified as high-impact options \citep{Wynes2017}.

A central question is whether direct emissions reductions in one domain are offset by higher emissions elsewhere---the rebound effect. Economic theory postulates that financial savings from reduced consumption in one domain free resources for substitution toward other goods, potentially eroding the climate benefit \citep{Berkhout2000,SorrellDimitropoulos2008,Hertwich2005,sorrell2020,Reimers2022}. Energy and emissions modelling frameworks routinely approximate these indirect effects using proportional or marginal re-spending calibrated on household expenditure patterns \citep{Chitnis2014,Bjelle2018,Grabs2015}, effectively treating freed resources as reallocating mechanically across a representative consumption bundle. Such rebound assumptions can substantially reduce the estimated climate benefits of lifestyle change in mitigation scenarios.

People avoid cars, air traveling, and meat for many reasons, including urban living, fear of flying, health, and animal welfare concerns. If a household avoids car ownership or air travel for environmental reasons, that same motivation may also discourage substitution toward other carbon-intensive alternatives. Identity theory provides a framework for this idea: individuals with strong environmental self-identity (ESI)---who see pro-environmental action as central to who they are---may behave more consistently across domains \citep{VanDerWerff2013,WhitmarshONeill2010,udall2021}. Positive spillover has been documented in survey and experimental studies \citep{Truelove2014,thogersen1999spillover}, but so has negative spillover where "doing good" in one domain license people to "doing bad" in another \citep{Merritt2010,Blanken2015}. Reviews conclude that the direction and magnitude of such effects depend on context, identity salience, and behaviour type, and that evidence from real-world comprehensive data is still limited \citep{Nilsson2017}.

Empirical tests of indirect relationships using expenditure data are rare because high-resolution spending records across all consumption domains are seldom available for population-based samples \citep{exadaktylos2021energy,trendl2023}. Using financial transaction data for a sample of environmentally motivated users of a carbon-calculator application, we found substantial net reductions and limited rebound, but the reliance on a self-selected green sample precluded inference about whether these patterns generalise to broader populations or whether the favourable indirect pattern was itself attributable to environmental motivation \citep{AnderssonNassen2023}. At the same time, certain domains---especially mobility---may exhibit trade-offs (e.g., between car ownership and air travel) that complicate simple spillover predictions \citep{ottelin2014greenhouse,Alcock2017}.

In this study, we link comprehensive transaction data, registry records, and survey responses for a random sample of 1{,}311 single-adult Swedish households. Our first question is whether emission savings in one domain are offset by higher emissions elsewhere. We examine three contrasts: living without a car, avoiding air travel, and following a non-meat diet. For each, we estimate regression-adjusted 12-month emissions differences and decompose them into focal-domain (direct) and non-focal (indirect) components. We then add two ESI-focused analyses.  The first---a values-consistent restraint test---asks whether environmental motivation helps explain the no-rebound pattern by checking whether indirect differences are more favorable at higher ESI. The second---a principled spillover test---tests whether lifestyle adoption itself triggers stronger behavioral spillover among high-ESI individuals (ESI\$\times\$lifestyle interaction), which would indicate that environmental motivation actively drives cross-domain restraint (Figs.\textbackslash{}ref\{fig\_main\}--\textbackslash{}ref\{fig\_category\}; Supplementary Information, SectionS7).

\section*{Results}
\subsection*{Sample characteristics}
The analytical sample comprised 1{,}311 single-adult households meeting data quality criteria (Methods). Registry and survey descriptive summaries are reported in Supplementary Information, Section~S2. The mean annual footprint from private consumption was approximately 5.37~tCO$_2$e per person.

\subsection*{Average direct and indirect differences}
Living without a car was associated with a regression-adjusted total emissions difference of $-$1.43~tCO$_2$e per person-year ($p < 0.001$). Avoiding air travel was associated with a total difference of $-$2.11~tCO$_2$e ($p < 0.001$). Following a non-meat diet was associated with a total difference of $-$0.56~tCO$_2$e ($p = 0.043$).

For car-free households, the direct component is $-$1.25~tCO$_2$e and the indirect component is $-$0.18~tCO$_2$e ($p = 0.41$). For non-flying households, the direct component is $-$1.82~tCO$_2$e and the indirect component is $-$0.29~tCO$_2$e ($p = 0.072$). For non-meat households, the direct component is $-$0.47~tCO$_2$e ($p < 0.001$), while the indirect component is small and non-significant ($-$0.09~tCO$_2$e, $p = 0.74$). Across the two mobility-related lifestyles, indirect effects are negative (in the spillover direction), though not individually significant at conventional levels. Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests confirm that the indirect pattern is distributional rather than driven by outliers, with significant left-shifts for car-free ($p = 0.037$) and non-flying households ($p = 0.003$); the corresponding shift for non-meat households is not significant ($p = 0.156$; Fig.~\ref{fig_violin}).

\subsection*{Proportional re-spending benchmark}
As a stylized benchmark, we approximate indirect emissions under proportional re-spending: the regression-adjusted reduction in focal-domain spending is multiplied by the mean outside-domain emissions intensity of the comparison group (Methods). 

For car-free households, the benchmark predicts an indirect increase of $+$0.249~tCO$_2$e from proportional reallocation of freed resources, whereas the observed indirect estimate is $-$0.182~tCO$_2$e, a gap of $-$0.431~tCO$_2$e. For non-flying households, the benchmark predicts $+$0.149~tCO$_2$e, against an observed $-$0.292~tCO$_2$e, a gap of $-$0.441~tCO$_2$e. For non-meat households, the benchmark predicts $-$0.018~tCO$_2$e (because focal-domain spending is higher rather than lower), while the observed indirect effect is $-$0.087~tCO$_2$e.

\subsection*{Add-on analysis: values-consistent restraint and principled spillover}
The average indirect differences above pool households with different levels of environmental motivation. We therefore run two add-on ESI analyses. The first is a values-consistent restraint test: we evaluate predicted indirect effects at low ($-$1~SD), mean, and high ($+$1~SD) ESI to examine whether stronger environmental motivation is associated with less rebound-like reallocation (Fig.~\ref{fig_esi}). At high ESI ($+$1~SD), indirect emission reductions are statistically significant for car-free households ($-$0.57~tCO$_2$e, $p = 0.027$) and non-flying households ($-$0.72~tCO$_2$e, $p < 0.001$); for non-meat households the high-ESI estimate is in the same direction but not significant ($-$0.44~tCO$_2$e, $p = 0.13$). At low ESI ($-$1~SD), indirect differences are small and non-significant: $+$0.20~tCO$_2$e ($p = 0.42$) for car-free, $+$0.14~tCO$_2$e ($p = 0.47$) for non-flying, and $+$0.27~tCO$_2$e ($p = 0.53$) for non-meat households. Thus, the values-consistent restraint test is directionally consistent with the idea that environmental motivation can help explain why strong rebound is not observed for this group. Results for low-ESI households is on the other hand coherent with rebound.

Category-level decomposition of spending shows that high-ESI reductions are broad-based with coherent patterns for the three lifestyle contrasts, whereas low-ESI rebound tends to concentrate in plausible substitution channels (Fig.~\ref{fig_category}; Supplementary Information, Section~S12). Supplementary Information, Section~S7 visualises these patterns as continuous predicted emission levels across the full ESI gradient.

The second is a principled spillover test---a stricter moderation check. Under mechanical rebound, freed resources are reallocated irrespective of motivation, so the ESI $\times$ lifestyle interaction on indirect outcomes should be zero. Under principled spillover, the interaction should be negative: among households with the same ESI level, adopters should show larger indirect reductions as ESI increases. In this sample, the ESI $\times$ lifestyle interaction terms on indirect spending are not individually significant for any of the three lifestyle contrasts (Supplementary Information, Section~S7).

The corresponding ESI $\times$ lifestyle interaction terms on indirect CO$_2$e are also not individually significant (Supplementary Information, Section~S7). Equivalently, formal tests of high- versus low-ESI indirect contrasts are not significant. Instead, the statistically significant high-ESI indirect reductions are driven mainly by the negative ESI main effect on indirect emissions ($p = 0.051$, $p = 0.006$, and $p < 0.001$ for the car, flying, and diet models, respectively; Supplementary Information, Section~S7). A stronger claim of principled spillover would require clear, robust negative ESI $\times$ lifestyle interactions on indirect outcomes. This criterion is not met here.

\section*{Discussion}
Economic rebound theory predicts that savings from one domain are mechanically reallocated across other goods and services \citep{Berkhout2000,sorrell2020}. The central finding of this work is that we do not observe respending in this random-sample setting. For car-free and non-flying households, indirect emissions are lower despite substantial direct savings, and benchmark comparisons suggest that proportional re-spending assumptions overstate offsetting. Our values-consistent restraint test suggests one plausible explanation: indirect patterns are more favorable at higher ESI. At the same time, low-ESI estimates are mostly near zero rather than showing broad, statistically robust rebound.

Our principled spillover test provides the stricter quasi-causal check: does adoption in one domain predict larger indirect reductions among higher-ESI households after controlling for ESI itself. In this sample, the ESI $\times$ lifestyle interactions on indirect emissions and spending are not significant, so principled spillover is not established. A larger sample may be needed to detect this mechanism if it exists. At the same time, the consistently negative ESI main effect on indirect emissions indicates that environmental motivation is associated with lower non-focal consumption in general, which remains highly relevant for how aggregate rebound should be represented.

More broadly, our results are consistent with our previous findings of limited rebound in a self-selected environmentally motivated sample \citep{AnderssonNassen2023} and lack of rebound for households who changed to a "green car" as opposed to others who changed car \citep{andersson2019estimating}. The present study extends that evidence by showing that the favourable indirect pattern concentrates among individuals with stronger environmental self-identity within a population-based sample. A robustness check re-estimating the interaction model on the non-random "green" subsample confirms that lifestyle main effects remain strongly negative (Supplementary Information, Section~S13).

The comparison between observed indirect differences and the proportional re-spending benchmark informs how rebound is represented in consumption-based emissions modelling. For both car-free and non-flying households, observed indirect emissions fall substantially below benchmark predictions (gaps of $-$0.43 and $-$0.44~tCO$_2$e respectively), suggesting that stylised proportional reallocation assumptions can overstate offsetting in mobility-related domains \citep{Bjelle2018,Chitnis2014,Grabs2015}. Distributional tests reinforce this, with significant left-shifts in residualized indirect emissions for car-free and non-flying households (Supplementary Information, Section~S8). For non-meat households, the benchmark and observed indirect values are both close to zero, consistent with the smaller financial savings involved.

These findings matter for quantitative modelling of demand-side mitigation. Many models impose budget closure with representative-agent demand systems, so savings in one category are mechanically redistributed according to average substitution elasticities \citep{Chitnis2013,Deaton1980,Gillingham2016,Niamir2020}. Our results suggest both that the respending assumption may be erroneous, and that averaging over motivation may misstate net indirect effects: households with stronger environmental motivation show systematically lower non-focal emissions. Models that condition rebound assumptions on different substantive lifestyle differences and adopter motivation may therefore yield more accurate projections of demand-side climate benefits \citep{Sorrell2007,Font_Vivanco2016,VandenBergh2011}.

Several limitations should be noted. Because the analysis is observational and cross-sectional, all estimates reflect population-based associations and should not be interpreted as causal effects of adopting a behaviour. Individuals who live without a car, avoid air travel, or follow non-meat diets likely differ from their comparison groups on dimensions not fully captured by observed controls, including preferences, built-environment access beyond population density and urbanity, and household history. The restriction to single-adult households avoids the complication of intra-household negotiation---in multi-adult households, pro-environmental preferences of one member may be moderated by the consumption priorities of others, potentially diluting the ESI--consumption relationship.

The Swedish cultural context deserves particular attention. Sweden exhibits comparatively high levels of environmental concern, alongside a long-standing public discourse emphasising individual responsibility for one´s carbon footprint \citep{eib_climate_2021,naturvardsverket_2023}. This is reflected in the emergence of the concept of \textit{flygskam} (flight shame), which originated in Sweden \citep{guardian_flygskam_2020}. Furthermore, prior research suggests relatively high levels of climate awareness and carbon literacy in the Swedish population, supported by strong public engagement with climate policy and mitigation efforts \citep{oecd_sweden_2025}. These norms may reinforce the behavioural consistency associated with environmental self-identity, making the ESI gradient observed here partly context-dependent. Whether similar patterns hold in societies with weaker environmental discourse is an important question for future research.

Transaction data offer near-complete coverage of monetary consumption but the automatic categorisation of purchases into consumption domains is imperfect: direct transfers or purchases at small or unusual retailers cannot always be classified. We mitigate this by requiring at least 90\% of each household's spending over the 12 month window to be successfully categorised, and sensitivity analyses confirm that lifestyle coefficients are stable across thresholds of 5--20\% uncategorised spending (Supplementary Information, Section~S6.2). As with all expenditure-based footprinting, carbon intensities are derived from broad input--output categories that cannot distinguish within-category variation---for instance between premium and budget products \citep{deHaan}---a limitation shared with household budget surveys. To the extent that environmentally motivated households select more expensive lower-emission products within a given category, this would work against detecting the indirect reductions we report here, making our estimates conservative. 

The 12-month observation window may not fully represent inter-annual variability, particularly for infrequent behaviours such as air travel. However, extended-window analysis (mean 21 months, range 12–39) reveals that the no-flying indirect effect actually strengthens and becomes clearly significant (p = 0.029) when adopters are identified over a longer period, indicating genuine cross-domain restraint rather than deferred consumption. Results for car-free and non-meat households are stable across the longer time windows (Supplementary Information, Section~S9).

Despite these limitations, the results provide rare empirical evidence that indirect emission patterns associated with low-carbon lifestyles depend on environmental motivation. We cannot determine whether environmental self-identity is itself shaped by underlying factors---such as green social norms, education, or cultural context---that independently influence consumption; however, the significant ESI main effect on indirect emissions indicates that higher environmental motivation is broadly associated with lower non-focal consumption, and the significant high-ESI indirect reductions for car-free and non-flying households show that this association is consequential in the context of lifestyle adoption. If lifestyle transitions occur at scale---whether driven by evolving social norms, policy interventions, or individual environmental concern---the net climate benefit will depend not only on the direct emissions avoided but also on the motivational composition of adopters: transitions driven by environmental concern may yield broader emission reductions than those driven by cost savings alone. Future research should examine whether similar identity-dependent patterns hold in multi-person households and across national contexts with different environmental discourse, and whether longitudinal designs tracking individuals before and after lifestyle transitions replicate the cross-sectional associations documented here.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Data and sample}

We link survey responses, official registry data, and 12 months of bank transaction records for a random sample of 1{,}311 Swedish adults. Consenting participants installed a secure app to share pseudonymised transaction data from their bank(s). The analysis is restricted to single-adult households to align transaction-recorded spending and survey-reported identity with a single consumption profile. Participants with more than 10\% uncategorised spending or incomplete 12-month time windows are excluded. Registry data provided demographic information (sex, age, income, and household composition), and surveys included environmental self-identity. Details on recruitment, ethics, sample construction, and benchmarking against the invited sampling frame are provided in Supplementary Information Sections S1--S2.

\subsection*{Carbon footprint estimation}
Transactions are mapped to consumption categories and linked to emission intensities derived from a Swedish environmentally extended input–output model \citep{andersson2020novel}. Annual emissions are computed as the sum of daily emission aggregates. Direct emissions are defined within the focal domain; indirect emissions are defined as total minus direct. Carbon footprint estimation and the mapping of behaviours to direct domains are described in Supplementary Information Section S3.

\subsection*{Statistical analysis}
We estimate linear models of 12-month emissions as a function of each lifestyle indicator, controlling for income, sex, age group, education, children, housing type, major city indicator, and log population density. Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors are used. Interaction terms test moderation by standardized environmental self-identity (ESI). As add-on analyses, we (i) evaluate predicted indirect effects at low ($-$1~SD), mean, and high ($+$1~SD) ESI (values-consistent restraint test) and (ii) test ESI $\times$ lifestyle interactions to assess whether the lifestyle effect itself varies with ESI after controlling for ESI main effects (principled spillover test).

To assess the prevalence of offsetting patterns, indirect emissions are residualized on covariates and their distribution examined by group. 

Inverse-probability weights---both for selection on observables into lifestyle groups and for differential participation across the invited sampling frame---are used as robustness checks; weighted estimates are similar to unweighted results (Supplementary Information, Section~S10).

\subsection*{Proportional re-spending benchmark}
For each lifestyle contrast, we estimate the regression-adjusted difference in focal-domain spending using the same model specification as for emissions, and interpret it as the mean financial saving attributable to the behaviour after conditioning on observed covariates. This saving is multiplied by the mean outside-domain emissions intensity of the comparison group—defined as total indirect emissions divided by total indirect spending—to approximate what indirect emissions would be if freed financial resources were reallocated proportionally across the comparison group's non-focal consumption bundle. The resulting benchmark represents an upper-bound estimate of indirect emissions under a reallocation assumption standard in modelling applications \citep{Bjelle2018,Chitnis2014,grabs2015rebound}.
