Dear Editor,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, ``Low-carbon lifestyles show broad
emission reductions without automatic rebound,'' for consideration as an Article
in \textit{Nature Climate Change}.

A core assumption in demand-side mitigation modelling is that financial savings from low-carbon choices are re-spent on other emitting goods, eroding the climate benefit. Inherited from the analysis of energy-efficiency gains, the assumption has never been tested against population-based consumption data. Using high-resolution bank-transaction records linked to administrative registers and survey responses for a random sample of 1,296 Swedish single-adult households, we provide the first such test, spanning the entire household consumption bundle.

We find no evidence of automatic rebound: car-free and flight-free households emit \textit{less}, not more, outside the focal domain despite large financial savings, with observed indirect emissions falling significantly below standard proportional re-spending benchmarks. Moreover, the broadest reductions occur among households with stronger environmental self-identity---implying that the net climate benefit of lifestyle transitions depends on motivational composition, not avoided direct emissions alone.

These findings bear directly on how rebound is handled in mitigation models and scenarios: for mobility-related lifestyle change, the full direct emission saving is a better guide to the climate benefit than estimates discounted by assumed re-spending. The results sits at the interface of behavioural science, industrial ecology, and mitigation modelling, and we believe it will engage the broad, policy-oriented readership of \textit{Nature Climate Change}.

The work is original and not under consideration elsewhere. We have not had prior discussions with a \textit{Nature Climate Change}
editor about this work.
